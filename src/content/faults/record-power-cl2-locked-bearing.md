## The symptom pattern

The lathe is running, then it isn't: the spindle stops dead mid-turn and
won't move again. Try to turn it by hand and there's no give at all — the
main bearing has locked solid on the spindle. It's an alarming way for a
machine to fail, because a seized bearing gives no warning and no partial
symptom beforehand; one pass it's turning normally, the next it's stopped.

## What's actually happening

Record Power's own knowledge base treats this as a known, named fault on the
CL2 family (shared with the CL3 and CL4), not a mystery failure. The main
spindle bearing has seized inside its race — from wear, from contamination
working its way in over time, or simply from age — and locked the spindle
solid rather than failing gradually. It reads like a catastrophic mechanical
failure. In practice it's a bearing that needs freeing, inspecting, and
either reusing or replacing, using a procedure Record Power publish
specifically for this symptom.

## Working through it

Isolate the lathe at the socket before doing anything else — everything from
here on is hands directly on the spindle and headstock.

The bearing is held by two locking rings, and the release order matters:
outer ring first, then the inner ring. With both released, tap the end of the
spindle with a rubber mallet. That's usually enough to break a seized bearing
free without resorting to a puller or heat, which risk damaging the spindle
or the bearing housing if used first.

Once it's moving, take the inner locking ring off completely and the bearing
slides straight out along the keyway — no special tooling needed. This is the
point to actually look at it rather than assume the worst: check the inside
of the bearing for heavy ridging or uneven wear. A bearing showing either of
those is finished and needs replacing. One that looks clean and even can
usually go straight back in.

## The fix

Refitting is the reverse of removal but in a specific order: tighten the
inner locking ring first, then the outer ring. Getting that sequence backwards
is an easy way to end up with a bearing that isn't seated correctly, so it's
worth following exactly.

Record Power also give a concrete way to check the setting is right rather
than guessing: put the lathe on middle speed, start it, and press stop
immediately. The belt — not the spindle — should turn 4 revolutions before it
comes to rest. That's their stated benchmark for a correctly set-up bearing,
and it's worth doing before putting the machine back into use, whether the
bearing was reused or replaced.

## Verdict

Worth doing properly rather than rushing. A locked bearing looks like the
kind of failure that would write off a machine, but Record Power's own procedure
treats it as routine maintenance: free it, look at it, and only replace what
the inspection actually condemns. The parts cost is modest against the
machine, and the belt-revolution check at the end means you're not left
guessing whether the reassembly actually took.
