## The symptom pattern

The complaint is usually put the same way: there is too much play in the
headstock spindle, and the vibration that comes with it makes the lathe
difficult to turn on. Cuts that used to come off cleanly now chatter, and a
finish that once needed a light sanding now needs work.

It reads like a machine reaching the end of its life. On a CL3 it usually
isn't.

## What's actually happening

Record Power's own answer to this question is worth stating plainly, because
it changes the whole outlook: the CL3's headstock bearings are designed to be
adjusted, specifically so that play appearing as they wear in use can be taken
back out. The detailed procedure is in the product manual. In other words,
some spindle movement developing over years of turning is anticipated by the
design and has a documented remedy that costs nothing.

That is the good news. The catch is that "play in the spindle" is what a lot
of different faults *feel* like from the handles, and adjusting bearings that
were never the problem wastes an afternoon and can leave the spindle worse
than it started.

## Working through it

Isolate the machine at the socket first — this is all hands-on-the-spindle
work and there is no reason for it to be live.

Take the workpiece off and test the spindle directly. Grip the spindle nose
and try to move it radially, up and down and side to side, then axially, in
and out along its own axis. Those two are worth separating: they feel
different and they are not the same adjustment.

Then do exactly the same test everywhere else the tool is supported, because
this is where most of these end. A banjo that has stopped clamping properly,
a toolrest stem that is a loose fit in it, or a tailstock barrel with slack in
it all produce chatter and vibration that a turner naturally reads as spindle
play. They are far more common than worn headstock bearings and much quicker
to fix.

Next, rule out the work itself. An out-of-balance blank shakes the whole
machine no matter how good the bearings are, and so does a chuck that is not
seated square on the spindle. So does a bench or stand that flexes — a sound
lathe on a springy stand feels exactly like a lathe with a loose spindle.

There is one symptom worth separating out completely. If the real complaint is
a spiral pattern running along finished spindle work, Record Power point
somewhere else entirely: their answer is that the most likely cause is that
the centres are out of alignment, and that tool sharpness and lathe speed are
the next things to check. Bearing wear is not their first suspect for that
one, and stripping into the headstock to chase it would be the wrong move.

Last of all, with the drive belt slackened off, turn the spindle by hand. A
bearing that is genuinely finished feels rough, notchy or noisy through the
rotation. One that simply has clearance turns smoothly and just moves.

## The fix

If the movement really is in the headstock and the bearings turn smoothly,
the fix is the adjustment described in the CL3 manual. Follow that procedure
rather than a general lathe guide — it is written for this headstock, and
bearing preload is the kind of thing where over-tightening does real damage.
Adjust to remove the play and no further, then check the spindle still turns
freely by hand before the belt goes back on.

If adjustment no longer takes the play up, or the bearings are rough or noisy
when turned by hand, they have gone past adjustment and need replacing.

## Verdict

Worth fixing, comfortably. The CL3 is a substantial cast-iron machine and
Record Power still support lathes decades old, so a headstock with adjustable
play is not a reason to move it on.

Realistically, most owners who work through the checks above find the movement
was in the banjo, the toolrest or the tailstock and never reach the bearings
at all. That is the cheapest possible outcome and it is also the most likely
one — which is exactly why the headstock should be the last thing you open,
not the first.
