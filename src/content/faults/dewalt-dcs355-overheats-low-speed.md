## The symptom pattern

You've been working the multi-tool at a gentle, careful pace — a light
squeeze on the trigger for a delicate sanding pass or a controlled cut —
and partway through it starts running hot and losing power. It feels like
exactly the moment you'd expect a tool to fail. DeWalt's own manual
describes this specific pattern directly, rather than leaving it as an
unexplained symptom.

## What DeWalt's own manual says

The DCS355's trigger is fully variable: "The further the trigger switch is
depressed the faster the tool will operate." That means a careful, partial
squeeze — exactly the technique many people use for delicate work — runs
the motor at its lower speed range. DeWalt's maintenance section addresses
what that does over time in so many words: "If the tool overheats,
especially when used at low speed, set the speed to maximum and run it
with no load for 2–3 minutes to cool the motor. Avoid prolonged usage at
very low speeds."

The same section connects a second factor to the same overload: "Excessive
force will reduce the working efficiency and cause motor overload.
Replacing the accessory regularly will maintain optimum working
efficiency." A blunted blade or a worn-through sanding sheet needs more
force to do the same job, which pushes the motor harder on top of whatever
the trigger speed is already asking of it.

## Working through it

Think back to how you were running the trigger when it started running
hot — a light, sustained partial squeeze matches the exact condition the
manual names. Stop the cut, release the trigger, then press it fully for
maximum speed and run the tool with no load — nothing touching the
workpiece — for the full 2–3 minutes DeWalt specifies, not a token few
seconds. While it's cooling, it's worth checking the accessory: if the
blade's gone blunt or the sanding sheet is worn through, swap it, since
the extra force needed to cut with a tired accessory adds directly to the
overload the manual describes. Once it's cooled and the accessory's fresh,
go back to the job favouring a higher trigger position where the work
allows it.

## Verdict

This is explicitly not treated as a fault in DeWalt's own documentation —
it's a named thermal limit with a specific, free recovery procedure, not a
part to replace or a repair to book. Keeping accessories sharp and
avoiding long stretches at a light partial-trigger speed should stop it
coming back. If the tool keeps overheating after a genuine full-speed,
no-load cool-down with a fresh accessory fitted, that's a different
problem from the one this specific guidance covers.
