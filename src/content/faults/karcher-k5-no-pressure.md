## The symptom pattern

The machine starts, the motor sounds healthy, and water comes out of the
lance — just not with any force. Sometimes it is a steady but feeble jet
that will not shift anything; sometimes the pressure surges and drops in a
rhythm, strong for a second and then soft. Owners often report it appearing
after the washer has been stored over winter, or after a job where the water
supply was drawn through a long, narrow hose.

The instinct is to assume the pump has worn out. It usually has not.

## What's actually happening

A pressure washer pump is a positive-displacement device: it can only
pressurise the water it is actually given, and it needs that water solid and
continuous. Three things break that.

The first is **restriction upstream** — a tap not fully open, a kinked or
undersized supply hose, or the small sieve in the water inlet connection
choked with grit. Starve the pump of flow and it cannot fill each stroke
completely, and the pressure it produces collapses.

The second is **restriction downstream**, and on a K5 that means the
high-pressure nozzle. The nozzle orifice is what converts pump pressure into
a working jet; partially block it with grit or limescale and you get a poor,
spitting spray from a pump that is working perfectly.

The third is **air**. Air is compressible in a way water is not, so a pump
with air trapped in it spends each stroke squashing a bubble instead of
moving a slug of water. This is why the symptom so often shows up on the
first run of the season, and why Kärcher's manual has a specific venting
procedure for it.

Only after all three are cleared does the pump itself come into question, and
the usual culprit there is the bypass valve — the part that recirculates
water when the trigger is released. Owner teardowns consistently describe the
plastic insert in the pump head swelling against oxidised aluminium and
holding that valve open, so the machine recirculates when it should be
pressurising.

## Working through it

Do the free checks in order and resist skipping ahead. Tap and hose first,
because it is the most common and the least interesting. Then the inlet
sieve: flat pliers, straight out, rinse, back in. Then the nozzle, cleared
with a needle from behind and flushed from the front. Then vent the machine
by running it for up to two minutes with the high-pressure hose off, until
the water leaving the outlet runs clear of bubbles.

The lance swap is the single most useful diagnostic on the list, because it
splits the machine in half. Pressure with no lance but not with one tells you
everything you need to know without taking a single fastener out.

Unplug the machine and release the trigger to dump stored pressure before
disconnecting anything under the bonnet — this is mains electricity sitting
next to a water circuit, and it deserves the respect.

## Verdict

Worth working through properly, because the cheap causes are also the likely
ones. A sieve, a nozzle and a vent cycle fix most of these outright and cost
nothing. Freeing a sticking bypass valve is a realistic home repair on a
machine you like. A full pump head assembly is the point at which the price
of the part and the price of a new K5 need putting side by side before you
commit.
