## The symptom pattern

You release the trigger and the blade keeps turning. On a saw with an
automatic electric brake that feels alarming, because the whole point of the
brake is that the blade stops promptly rather than coasting down. Owners
usually notice it as a change rather than an event: the saw used to stop
almost as soon as the trigger came off, and now it runs on.

The first thing worth doing is checking whether anything is actually wrong.
DeWalt's own figure for the DWS780 is that the automatic blade brake stops
the blade in **under 10 seconds**. That is the specification, not an ideal —
a saw that takes a couple of seconds is working as designed, and a blade
that free-coasts for half a minute is not.

## What's actually happening

The electric brake is not a mechanical pad. It works by reversing the
circuit through the motor so that the motor itself resists rotation, and it
does that through the same carbon brushes that feed the motor in normal
running. Those brushes are held against the commutator by springs.

That detail is what makes this fault a maintenance issue rather than an
electrical mystery. As the carbon wears down, the springs run out of travel
and stop pressing the brushes firmly onto the commutator. Normal running
tolerates a poor contact better than braking does, so the brake is often the
first thing to go — the saw still cuts perfectly well while stopping badly,
which is exactly the combination that makes people suspect the switch or the
motor rather than a consumable.

DeWalt is unusually specific about the threshold: at approximately **12.7 mm
(1/2 inch)** of remaining carbon, the springs no longer exert pressure and
the brushes must be replaced.

## Working through it

Unplug the saw first. This is a check on the motor's electrical contacts, and
a mitre saw trigger is easy to catch with a sleeve while you are leaning over
the housing.

The brushes come out through caps on the motor housing, which is the whole
reason they are treated as a serviceable item rather than a teardown. Take
both out, keep them the right way round so each goes back to the commutator
face it has already bedded into, and measure the carbon. Below the limit,
they are the answer. Check the second one even if the first is obviously
finished — they wear at different rates and the brake needs both.

If both brushes are comfortably within limits, the honest thing is to stop.
The brake circuit also runs through the trigger switch assembly, and
diagnosing that is a different job from swapping a consumable. Sources on
what specifically fails there are thinner than the manual's brush guidance,
so this entry does not pretend to a confident answer beyond ruling the
brushes in or out.

## Verdict

Worth fixing when it is the brushes. They are inexpensive, designed to be
replaced without opening the tool, and replacing them restores the brake
along with the motor contact.

Two conditions from the manual are worth taking seriously rather than
skipping. The first is that you fit **identical DeWalt brushes** — the manual
states that the correct grade of brush is essential for the electric brake to
operate properly, which means a generic brush of the right physical size is
not necessarily the right part. The second is the run-in: the saw should be
run at no load for **10 minutes** to seat new brushes before you rely on it.

If the brushes measure fine, the calculation changes. At that point you are
looking at a switch or motor fault on a saw that still cuts accurately, and
the sensible move is to get a repair price and set it against what a
replacement costs — a working brake is a safety feature, so a saw that has
lost it is not something to simply carry on using while you decide.
